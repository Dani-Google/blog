Claude's "ADHD Executive Function Mode" Is the Best AI Coding Trick I've Seen

Short answer: I have a focus problem. I know it, I have named it before, and I have watched it eat whole days: I sit down to work, stare at the task, and somehow end up everywhere except inside the work.

I have a focus problem. I know it, I have named it before, and I have watched it eat whole days: I sit down to work, stare at the task, and somehow end up everywhere except inside the work. So when I found a way to make Claude act like an executive-function coach for my brain, I tested it the same week — and I am keeping it.

One honest thing first. Claude does not ship a button called "ADHD Executive Function Mode." There is no toggle hidden in settings. What exists is better: a set of prompts that turn Claude into that mode. You describe the exact way your brain is stuck, and Claude builds the scaffolding your brain will not build on its own — tiny steps, check-ins, rewards, realistic time maps. That is the whole trick, and the seven prompts below are how I use it.

1. The Task Paralysis Shatterer

This is the one I reach for most. I am staring at a task and cannot start — not because it is hard, but because starting feels enormous.

"I am staring at [Task] and can't start. Break this down into 'Ridiculously Small' steps that take less than 1 minute each. Give me the first step and tell me exactly where to put my hands to begin."

The magic is the last line. "Tell me exactly where to put my hands" removes the decision. I do not decide what to do first — Claude decides, and I just move my hands.

2. The Dopamine Menu Architect

Some days I am not tired, I am under-stimulated. My brain wants novelty and my task offers none, so I drift.

"I am feeling under-stimulated. Create a 'Dopamine Menu' for me with 5-minute 'Appetizers' (quick movement), 20-minute 'Entrees' (deep work), and 10-minute 'Sides' (creative play) to keep my brain engaged."

I pick one appetizer, one entree, one side, and the day has a shape. The menu idea works because it treats stimulation as fuel, not as a distraction to fight.

3. The "Body Doubling" Simulator

Body doubling is simple: having someone present while you work makes it easier to stay on task. I do not always have a person. Claude fills the chair.

"Act as my virtual body double for the next 30 minutes. I will tell you what I'm working on, and I want you to check in every 10 minutes to ask for a status update and keep my focus anchored."

Knowing a check-in is coming in ten minutes changes how I spend those ten minutes. That is the entire mechanism, and it works.

4. The Context-Switching Guide

Finishing one thing and starting another is where I lose the most time. My brain stays in the old task's energy and refuses to move.

"I just finished [Task A] and need to start [Task B], but my brain is stuck. Design a 3-minute 'Mental Palate Cleanser' routine to help me transition between these two different types of energy."

Three minutes of a deliberately different activity — a walk, water, stretching — and the switch stops feeling like a wall.

5. The Interest-Based Filter

Boring admin work is my kryptonite. The fix is not discipline; it is connection. If the task touches something I already care about, my brain stops resisting.

"I have a boring administrative task: [Task]. Help me gamify this by connecting it to my current hyper-fixation: [Interest]. Create a 'Quest' structure where finishing the task unlocks a reward."

Claude builds the quest, I play it. The reward at the end is real — I choose it in advance, and I only get it when the task is done.

6. The Time-Blindness Auditor

I think a project will take 20 minutes. It takes two hours. Every time. The problem is not laziness — it is the three hidden sub-tasks I never counted.

"I think [Project] will take 20 minutes, but it usually takes 2 hours. Help me 'Time-Map' this by identifying the 3 hidden sub-tasks I always forget to account for so I can set a realistic deadline."

After a week of time-mapping, my estimates got honest. Honest estimates mean fewer missed deadlines, which means less guilt, which means more focus. It compounds.

7. The Executive Function Externalizer

My brain holds too many open loops — worries, reminders, half-formed tasks — and the noise drowns out the work. The fix is to dump it all out and sort it outside my head.

"My brain is full of 'Open Loops.' I will dump everything I'm worried about below. Categorize these into 'Now,' 'Later,' and 'Trash,' and then write a 1-sentence 'Actionable Next Step' for only the 'Now' items."

I do this every morning now. Five minutes of dumping, and the day starts quiet.

How I run a day with these

My routine is simple: I start with prompt 7 (dump the loops), pick the first task with prompt 1 (shatter it), work in 30-minute blocks with prompt 3 (body doubling), and use prompt 4 (palate cleanser) between tasks. Prompts 2, 5 and 6 are my rescue tools for the specific ways the day goes wrong.

None of this cured my focus problem. It gave me a system for the days my brain will not cooperate — and those were the days I used to lose entirely. If your brain works like mine, try one prompt today. Start with the first one. Tell Claude where to put your hands, and then just move them.
