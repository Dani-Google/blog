"Software Is Dead" — Not Quite. But This Is What It Actually Means for Creators

I keep coming back to one line Mark Cuban said recently: software is dead, because everything is going to be customized to your unique utilization.

Read it again. Not "software is changing." Dead. Because the old model — one app, built once, sold to a million people who all bend their workflow to fit it — is being replaced by something else entirely: tools built for one person, one job, one exact way of working.

That is not a prediction about the far future. It is a job description for right now.

"Software Is Dead" — What That Actually Means

For thirty years, the deal was simple. A company built a product, you learned its buttons, and you worked the way it wanted you to work. The software was the boss.

AI flips that deal. Now the software bends to you. You describe what you need, in your own words, and the tool shapes itself around your problem, your files, your process. The question is no longer "which app do I buy?" It is "who is going to build the thing that fits me?"

Cuban's answer is blunt: somebody has to do it for them. And that somebody is the biggest hiring opportunity of the decade.

The Biggest Hiring Opportunity of the Decade

Here is the part most people will miss. The opportunity is not "learn to code." It is not "become a prompt engineer." It is this: become the person who can take a real business problem and turn it into a working, customized solution using AI tools — fast, cheap, and without waiting for a software company to ship a feature.

Every small business owner, every freelancer, every team lead has a workflow that no off-the-shelf app fits properly. They have been waiting for someone to build their version. AI made that build possible in days instead of months. The people who learn to do it will not be looking for work. Work will be looking for them.

Most People Use AI Like a Better Google

And here is the honest part. Most people are going to miss it — because of how they use AI today.

They open a chatbot, ask a question, get an answer, and close the tab. A better search engine. A faster summary. A slightly smarter autocomplete. Useful, yes. But that is using a power tool as a hammer.

The gap between the two groups is not talent. It is not money. It is one thing: knowing what your tools can actually do.

The Real Gap: Knowing What Your Tools Can Do

I learned this the hard way. For months I used Claude the same way everyone does — paste a question, get a reply. Then I started opening the features I had been ignoring, and the same tool turned into something else entirely.

Claude Code turns a plain-English description into working software. Projects keep your files, instructions, and context in one place so you stop re-explaining yourself every session. Skills let you save a multi-step workflow once and reuse it forever. MCP connections let Claude reach into your other tools — your files, your calendar, your data — and work with them directly. Artifacts turn a chat answer into a working page, document, or app you can actually use.

None of this is hidden. It is all sitting in the interface, one click away. Most people never touch it.

How I Am Closing the Gap

So this is what I do now, and it is the simplest system I have found. Every week, I pick one feature I have never used and I force myself to do a real task with it — not a demo, a real task with a real deadline. One week it was Claude Code building a page. Another week it was a Skill that automated a workflow I used to do by hand.

Each feature I learn is not a trick. It is a new kind of work I can offer someone. And every piece of work I ship becomes proof that I can build the customized version — the one no off-the-shelf app can match.

Your Move This Week

If you take one thing from this, take this: do not learn AI in general. Learn one feature deeply. Open Claude, pick the feature you have been scrolling past, and build one real thing with it this week. Then pick the next one.

Software is dead. The people who build what comes next will not be the ones who waited for the perfect app. They will be the ones who learned to make the tool do the actual work.
